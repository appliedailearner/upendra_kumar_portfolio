MIT's own research found that **95% of enterprise generative AI pilots deliver no measurable P&L impact.** Not because the models are weak — because nobody can say which agent touched what, who approved it, or what it cost.

Microsoft's answer is the **Citadel Governance Hub**: a four-layer blueprint (gateway → control plane → agent identity → security fabric) that turns AI agents from anonymous scripts into governed, auditable, cost-attributed citizens of the enterprise.

I spent this week pulling it apart. Three things stood out:

🛑 **The market is real, not hype.** Gartner projects AI governance platform spend more than doubling — from $492M in 2026 to over $1B by 2030 — as fragmented regulation reaches 75% of the world's economies. This isn't a compliance nice-to-have anymore.

🛑 **The architecture is genuinely good.** Hub-and-spoke, not fully centralized — one gateway enforcing identity, policy, and cost rules on every agent call, while business units keep building inside their own spokes. It's the same pattern that works for Azure landing zones, pointed at a fleet of agents instead of subscriptions.

🛑 **The diagram leaves out the part that actually breaks governance programs.** The hub enforces policy — it doesn't write it. Somebody still has to own the risk taxonomy, decide what "approved" means per agent category, and translate a regulatory obligation into a rule the gateway can enforce. Skip that, and you've bought very good plumbing for a policy that doesn't exist yet.
