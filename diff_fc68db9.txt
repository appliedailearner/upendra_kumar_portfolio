-        <p>
-            The Model Router discussion should not be framed around which model to use. That is too narrow. A better architecture question is: <em>What AI operating model gives us the right balance of quality, cost, latency, compliance, observability, and business accountability?</em>
-        </p>
-        <p>
-            Common enterprise AI failure patterns include:
-        </p>
-        <ul>
-            <li>Every workflow defaults to the most expensive model out of developers' convenience.</li>
-            <li>Critical business decisions are routed to low-capability models to meet arbitrary budgets.</li>
-            <li>Model selection is buried inside deep application code, invisible to platform administrators.</li>
-            <li>Governance teams approve an AI use case but fail to regulate the model pool behind it.</li>
-            <li>Teams confuse dynamic model routing with AI platform governance.</li>
-        </ul>
-        
-        <div class="callout-box">
-            <h4><i class="fas fa-info-circle"></i> Architectural Mandate</h4>
-            The goal is not to use the biggest model. The goal is to use the right model, for the right task, with the right controls, placed inside a governed platform pattern.
-        </div>

-        <h2 id="how-it-works"><span class="gradient-text">How Azure AI Foundry Model Router Works</span></h2>
-        <p>
-            Model Router acts as a model decision layer. Instead of directly calling a fixed endpoint, an application calls the Model Router deployment. The router uses an internal classifier to estimate prompt complexity before selecting a model from the configured subset ΓÇö the routing logic is managed by the platform and is not directly configurable by the developer. The selected model is disclosed in the API response <code>model</code> field, giving downstream observability tooling a clear audit signal.
-        </p>
