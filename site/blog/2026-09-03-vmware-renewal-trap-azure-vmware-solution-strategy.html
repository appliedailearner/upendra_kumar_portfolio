The renewal quote lands on a Tuesday. The number is a multiple of last year's — often 3×, and in the cases quoted to regulators, far more. Somewhere in the next 30 minutes, someone will say "let's just lift it to Azure VMware Solution." That sentence is a strategy decision wearing a migration task's clothes — and getting it wrong is a multi-year, multi-million-dollar commitment.

Written by Upendra Kumar — Cloud Solution Architecture leadership, focused on Azure migration and landing-zone strategy. This analysis maps Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework guidance for Azure VMware Solution onto the decision as a platform owner actually has to make it: with a budget, a board, and a renewal clock.

Strategic Alignment & ROI

Executive Impact Summary

The Business Problem

Post-Broadcom VMware renewals vary widely. European cloud providers (CISPE) reported renewal increases of 800–1,500% to the EU Commission; AT&T cited 1,050% in a US court filing. Most enterprises don't see numbers that extreme — but perpetual licensing has ended, the catalog has collapsed into subscription bundles, and the renewal is now a board-level event rather than a procurement one. It forces a platform decision the roadmap was going to defer.

The Strategic Play

Treat the renewal as a trigger for a platform review, not another licensing decision. Azure VMware Solution should win where workload constraints, migration risk and business timing point to it — an aggressive datacenter-exit date, VMware-specific dependencies, high refactoring risk — not simply because it is the easiest migration technology. Native Azure often fits better where modernization is already funded or the workload has no VMware dependency. And AVS no longer sidesteps Broadcom: since 1 November 2025 the VCF license is bought directly from Broadcom as a separate subscription.

Where the Value Comes From

A Microsoft-commissioned Forrester Total Economic Impact study (2026) models a positive multi-year return for a composite AVS adopter — driven mostly by avoided hardware refresh, datacenter exit, and redeployed operations time, not by cheaper compute. Commissioned studies are directional, not a guarantee. But the pattern holds: the value of an AVS move is in retiring the hidden estate and buying time to modernize on a funded schedule — not in the per-VM rate.

The Renewal Quote Is a Forcing Function

For a decade, "what do we do about VMware" was a question you were allowed to not answer. The hardware ran. The team knew every quirk of vCenter by heart. The perpetual licenses were bought and paid for years ago. Modernization sat in the backlog behind every revenue feature, lost every planning round, and no one was blamed for the delay. That was the arrangement.

Broadcom changed the arrangement. Perpetual licensing has been retired in favour of subscriptions. The product catalog has collapsed into a small set of bundles (VCF and VVF). The minimum subscription order moved to 72 cores. For a small estate of modest dual-socket hosts, that floor alone can mean licensing more cores than you actually run — before any per-core price change.

This is not a handful of unlucky customers venting:

1,050%
the VMware price increase AT&T cited in a US court filing against Broadcom. It is an outlier, not a median. But CISPE's members reported renewal increases of 800–1,500% to the EU Commission, and Broadcom closed the VMware Cloud Service Provider Program in January 2026. The renewal stopped being predictable.

The exit most teams reach for first — lift it to Azure VMware Solution and sort the licensing out later — has quietly picked up an expiry date of its own, courtesy of Microsoft's 2025–27 AVS licensing changes. That story is a few sections down, and it moves the deadline closer.

Here's the trap. A renewal deadline creates urgency, and urgency collapses a strategy question into a procurement question. The room stops asking "where should these workloads live for the next seven years" and starts asking "what's the fastest way to not pay this invoice." Those are different questions with different right answers.

Microsoft's Cloud Adoption Framework is unusually blunt about where Azure VMware Solution fits: it's the right choice "when the business value of moving to Azure is clear, and the cost, risk, or time required to redesign applications aren't acceptable." That's the sentence to anchor the whole decision on.

Composite Scenario

The following is a composite, illustrative walkthrough representative of a pattern seen repeatedly across datacenter-exit programs — not a specific client engagement. Figures are round and directional.

  • Fictional company: Meridian Logistics
  • The estate: ~340 VMs on vSphere, two colocation halls, a hardware refresh due in 14 months
  • The trigger: a VMware renewal quote roughly 6× the prior term, and a colo contract that auto-renews for three years if not cancelled 180 days out
  • The pressure: the CFO wants the capital-refresh line gone from the plan; the CIO wants no Sev-1 during peak season

Meridian's platform owner did the thing that feels responsible: she asked her team for a migration plan. Two weeks later she had a Gantt chart for lifting 340 VMs into Azure VMware Solution. What she didn't have was an answer to the question the board would actually ask — why AVS and not native Azure, and what does this commit us to?

The Wrong Question

"How do we migrate?" is the wrong first question, because it has already chosen the destination. The right first move is to interrogate the destination itself — because lift-and-shift to AVS isn't a neutral parking spot on the way to somewhere better. It's a fork in a roadmap you'll be living with for years.

Say yes to AVS and you've said yes to three things, whether or not anyone spelled them out in the meeting:

  • You stay in the VMware business. vSphere, vSAN, NSX — the same stack, the same daily operations, just running on hardware Microsoft racks and patches instead of hardware you bought.
  • Your bill is set by the host, not the workload. AVS charges per AVS host, never per VM — so a half-empty cluster costs exactly what a full one does, and every point of consolidation you skip before you migrate is money you keep paying every month afterward.
  • Broadcom is still your vendor. You still buy the VCF portable subscription — now straight from Broadcom, not folded into the Azure price. AVS changes who racks the servers; it doesn't end the Broadcom relationship. It splits your VMware estate across two suppliers, each with its own contract and its own renewal clock.

None of this is an argument against AVS. It's an argument for choosing it with your eyes open — with the case for not doing it sitting on the same table.

And "AVS vs. refactor" isn't the whole board either. The full slate is wider: the other hyperscalers' VMware services (AWS, Google Cloud, OCI), re-hypervisoring to Hyper-V or another platform, Nutanix on Azure, or a deliberately smaller on-premises footprint. Most of those are worse fits for a renewal-clock timeline — but a platform owner who hasn't named them on a slide will get asked why in the board meeting, not before it.

One more thing the Gantt chart won't show you: the portable VCF subscription is bought directly from Broadcom, on Broadcom's terms and timeline. Through 2025 those quotes were inconsistent — slow to arrive, re-priced, occasionally refused. If your AVS plan depends on a renewal-window cutover, get the licensing commitment in writing before you cancel anything on the old platform.

The "safe" route is on a clock too

Teams pick AVS partly to make the Broadcom licensing problem disappear. It doesn't disappear — it just moves. AVS is not going away, but the all-in-one license is gone, and every existing deployment now has a deadline.

Already happened

  • 15 Oct 2025. Your included vDefend firewall allowance was frozen at that day's usage. Use more and you pay extra.
  • 1 Nov 2025. Microsoft stopped bundling the VMware license with new AVS hosts. You now buy it straight from Broadcom — a "portable VCF" subscription at roughly $175–$240 per CPU core per year, with Broadcom (not Microsoft) setting future prices.

Hard deadlines

  • 31 Oct 2026. Existing pay-as-you-go AVS on the old bundled license must switch to buy-direct. Miss it and Microsoft warns of "risk of service suspension" for any capacity above your Broadcom core count.
  • 30 Aug 2027. Same switch for reserved (1- and 3-year) AVS — convert the license or move those workloads off AVS.

The result: one AVS estate, two vendors to manage — Microsoft for the hardware, Broadcom for the license, each with its own bill and renewal date. And you can't dodge it by choosing AWS or Google instead: both made the identical change. The direct line to Broadcom follows VMware wherever it runs. Put both vendors in the cost model — and in the exit plan.

Architect's Corner: Moving the House vs. Rebuilding It

AVS: Move the House

Same house, new lot. You lift it off the foundation, set it down somewhere else, and everyone sleeps in their own bed that night. Nothing about how you live changes — which is the point, and also the catch. The dated kitchen and the drafty windows came along for the ride. That renovation is still coming, and now you're doing it on rented land.

  • Weeks to months, not years. HCX moves VMs in bulk and stretches the network so the cutover barely shows.
  • Your team's skills, runbooks, and dashboards all still work on day one.

Refactor: Rebuild It

Demolish and build new to code — native Azure VMs, managed databases, PaaS. What you get back is cheaper to run and actually suited to where it sits. What it costs you: months in temporary housing, a budget that behaves like a project instead of a bill, and a change request every time you open a wall and find something load-bearing.

  • Measured in quarters. Every application is its own small project.
  • The biggest long-term payoff — if the renewal clock leaves you room to do it, which it rarely does.

The grown-up answer is almost always both, in order: move the house now to stop the bleeding, then renovate one room at a time on a schedule you control and a budget you've planned. AVS is the bridge. It was never meant to be the destination.

When AVS Is the Right Call — and When It Isn't

The Cloud Adoption Framework lists five situations that point toward AVS. Treat them as a checklist, not a menu — the more that are true for you, the stronger the case:

You're on a deadline to get out

A lease ends, a datacenter closes, or another cloud is pushing you off — and the date won't move. VMware HCX can migrate VMs in bulk and, where the network design permits, extend Layer 2 so IP-address changes can be reduced or deferred during the cutover. That can compress the plan into the window you actually have.

The apps only run on VMware

Business-critical systems depend on OS versions, middleware, or vendor-certified setups that no one has validated on plain Azure VMs. AVS keeps the exact VMware environment they're supported on.

The team is built around VMware

Years of vCenter know-how, runbooks, monitoring, backups, and automation. AVS lets the team keep working the way it already does while it picks up Azure skills at a pace the business can take.

You want Azure's services without rewriting apps

AI, analytics, managed databases, security tooling — you want access now, not after a modernization project. Low-latency links to Azure services (plus Azure Arc for one control plane) let you adopt them one at a time.

You need a DR site without building one

AVS becomes the failover target for VMware running on-premises or in another cloud — real resilience without paying for a second physical datacenter.

The CAF is just as direct about when to send the workloads to native Azure instead. Lean native if:

  • You plan to modernize or refactor the applications in the near term anyway.
  • The workloads run well on Azure VMs with no VMware dependencies.
  • The VMware footprint is small enough to migrate directly to native services.
  • Your primary objective is long-term cloud optimization, not VMware continuity.
  • Most of the estate is new or could be built cloud-native from the start.

In those cases, the CAF notes, "moving directly to Azure IaaS or PaaS services often provides greater cloud-native benefits and can reduce long-term platform costs." AVS there is an expensive way to postpone a decision you've already made.

When the signals point native, the target is workload-specific, not one-size-fits-all. The common mappings:

Signal in the estate Native Azure target
Stateless or horizontally scalable app VM Scale Sets, AKS, App Service, or Container Apps
Windows / Linux VM with no VMware hooks Azure IaaS VMs, with Azure Hybrid Benefit for Windows and SQL licenses you already own
Databases running on VMs Azure SQL Managed Instance, Flexible Server, or Cosmos DB — retire the database VMs
File or object storage on vSAN Azure Files, Azure NetApp Files, or Blob storage
Bursty or seasonal demand Native autoscale — pay for consumption instead of fixed node capacity
DR and backup Azure Site Recovery and Azure Backup
New development Cloud-native PaaS from day one — never routed through VMware

For workloads that fit it, native Azure tends to lower long-term infrastructure dependency — consumption pricing, no dedicated-host floor, no separate VCF subscription, and lower exit friction. The trade is a real operating-model change and per-application migration and remediation effort, which is why a renewal clock rarely leaves room to do it all at once.

Four Paths, Not One Destination

For most large, heterogeneous VMware estates, the practical answer is a portfolio strategy rather than a single destination. Every workload takes one of four paths:

Retire

Unused, duplicated, or obsolete. Confirm with the business owner, then decommission — never migrate it.

Retain temporarily

Stays where it is for now — a short remaining lifespan, a pending divestment, or a dependency that clears soon. Revisit on a date.

Move to AVS

VMware-bound, deadline-driven, or too risky to refactor now. AVS is one possible landing zone — and, for a VMware-free target state, a time-boxed bridge with a funded exit.

Move or modernize to native Azure

No hard VMware dependency, or modernization already funded. Rehost to Azure IaaS, or re-platform to PaaS where the target architecture exists.

Assess each workload against: business criticality, technical dependencies, migration complexity, latency needs, licensing, supportability, modernization readiness, remaining application lifespan, security requirements, the migration deadline, and the business owner's appetite for change. The mix that results — not a single platform — is the strategy.

The Comparison That Makes AVS Look Like a Bad Deal

When the renewal quote is on the screen, one mistake gets made almost every time: someone pulls up the Azure VMware Solution price, sets it next to the price of a plain Azure VM, sees that AVS costs more per unit, and calls it a bad deal.

Wrong thing to compare. AVS isn't going up against a rack of Azure VMs. It's replacing your whole VMware platform — the servers, the vSphere and vSAN and NSX licenses, the datacenter or colo space they sit in, the DR site, and the hours your team spends patching and nursing all of it. The Cloud Adoption Framework says it in one line: measure AVS against the full cost of the platform you run today.

Line up that whole bill and the picture flips. Per unit of compute, AVS will never beat hardware you've already paid off — it was never going to. The savings are in the lines that disappear: the colo contract, the next hardware refresh, the half-idle DR site, the firmware-and-vCenter babysitting your admins stop doing.

Microsoft's commissioned Forrester study pins roughly 90% of its modelled return on just two of those — skipping the hardware refresh and walking away from the datacenter — plus admin time moved off maintenance. One more line that's easy to forget: AVS bundles free Extended Security Updates for out-of-support Windows Server and SQL Server. On-premises, you pay for those.

An illustrative model — build your own with real inputs

Round, directional, list-price, 3-year, ~36-host-equivalent estate. Not a client figure — a shape:

  • Stay (owned / colo): hardware refresh + post-Broadcom VCF renewal + two colo halls + platform-ops headcount + a second DR footprint.
  • AVS (3-year reserved hosts): host charges + VCF portable subscription + one-time migration cost — with the colo, the refresh, and most of the DR-site line removed entirely.

Public case-study territory for datacenter-exit scenarios of this shape runs from roughly break-even to a 30%+ three-year TCO reduction. Where you land is set almost entirely by how much colocation and DR-duplication cost you're actually removing — not by the Azure compute line. Small estate, cheap colo, no real DR site? The number gets thin fast.

One floor to know before you model: an AVS cluster has a three-host minimum, so the smallest production private cloud is three hosts regardless of how little you're running on it. Below roughly that footprint, native Azure IaaS is almost always the cheaper answer — which is exactly the CAF's "small VMware footprint" exclusion, expressed as a bill.

Two levers move the AVS number more than anything else, and both are yours to pull:

  • Host density. Because you pay per host, a cluster running at 45% utilization is burning roughly half your spend on air. Right-sizing and consolidation before migration is the highest-ROI work in the whole program.
  • Reserved instances. A 1- or 3-year reservation on AVS hosts changes the comparison materially against a fresh hardware capital outlay. If the workloads are staying put for three years — and if you're choosing AVS, they are — not reserving is leaving money on the table. Reservations also affect the licensing runway: an active VCF license-included reserved instance keeps its included license until the reservation expires or 30 August 2027, whichever comes first; any new reservation is BYOL from the start.

From Decision to Proposal: How You Get the Numbers

Everything to this point is the argument. What a board actually approves is the argument with a costed proposal stapled to it: host counts, a three-year TCO, a wave plan, and a defensible number for "why not just go native." In Azure, that deliverable comes out of one place — Azure Migrate.

What the assessment actually produces

Azure Migrate does three jobs, and they line up one-to-one with the questions a platform owner has to answer:

Dependency mapping

Agentless analysis reads TCP connections from the appliance and clusters related VMs into application groups — so you migrate "the SAP estate" as one unit instead of finding the undocumented dependency during the cutover weekend.

Right-sizing

Two assessment types on the same group: the AVS assessment sizes the number of hosts; the Azure VM assessment sizes each VM's SKU and disks. Both can size from 95th-percentile performance history rather than allocated capacity.

Side-by-side cost

Run both assessments plus the Business case report and you get exactly the comparison the board asks for: today's on-premises cost against native Azure against AVS, with Hybrid Benefit and reservations already priced in.

The AVS assessment is the output that anchors the proposal. Per workload group it returns a readiness verdict (Ready / Ready with conditions / Not ready), the number of hosts required, and — the useful part — the limiting factor that drives that count: CPU, memory, or storage. It also bakes in the management overhead for vCenter, NSX, and HCX (roughly 44 vCPU, 75 GB RAM, and 722 GB of storage before dedupe), a fixed tax on the first host that catches people sizing on a spreadsheet.

Host count isn't a fixed readout. A short list of settings moves it — and moves the bill — which is where an experienced assessor earns the engagement fee:

  • vSAN failure-tolerance policy (FTT / RAID). FTT=1 with RAID-1 mirroring roughly halves usable capacity; RAID-5 erasure coding keeps about 75%. On a storage-bound estate that's the gap between a four- and a five-host cluster.
  • CPU oversubscription and memory overcommit. 4:1 vCPU is a reasonable default for mixed workloads; SAP and latency-sensitive tiers need it tighter, which costs hosts.
  • Comfort factor. A buffer on the observed performance data — 1.3 is typical. Too low and you undersize into a Sev-1; too high and you're paying for air.
  • External storage. The assessment automatically models Azure NetApp Files when adding it lowers the host count by relieving a storage bottleneck — usually cheaper than buying entire hosts for disk you'll barely compute against.

The prerequisites nobody budgets time for

"Run Azure Migrate" sounds like something you do in the last week before the renewal date. It isn't. Performance-based sizing needs a representative sample of real load — one day at the absolute minimum, one to four weeks to trust the confidence rating — and that clock only starts once the plumbing is in place:

  • the Azure Migrate appliance deployed as a VMware OVA (one instance scales to roughly 10,000 VMs);
  • a read-only vCenter account — and for dependency and software inventory, that account also needs Guest Operations rights plus valid in-guest credentials loaded into the appliance, with VMware Tools running on the guests;
  • outbound connectivity from the appliance to Azure.

The shortcut — importing an RVTools export or the CSV template — produces a rough sizing in an afternoon, but with no dependency map, no software inventory, and no performance-confidence rating. It's a way to start the business case, not a substitute for letting the appliance watch real load.

The subscription trap that stalls programs

You can assess from any subscription. You cannot deploy from any subscription.

Azure Migrate discovery, dependency analysis, and every assessment run on any subscription — pay-as-you-go, Visual Studio, even a free trial. It's calculation and pricing only; nothing is provisioned. But requesting AVS host quota requires an Enterprise Agreement, a CSP / Azure Plan, or an MCA subscription. A standalone PAYG or Visual Studio subscription is not eligible — and teams routinely hit this the week they try to build, not the week they planned to.

The quota request is otherwise harmless: a free support ticket, capacity headroom only, up to five business days to land, and it expires in 30 days if nothing is deployed against it (reservation-backed quota doesn't expire). Raise it in week one, and enter the new absolute total you want for the SKU and region — not the increment.

The Gen1 vs Gen2 Decision You Make Once

Azure VMware Solution ships in two private-cloud generations. The vSphere, vCenter and NSX experience is broadly consistent, but the difference is more than networking. Gen2 changes the network attachment model, the host SKU, the vSAN storage architecture, and how the private cloud lands in your Azure design.

Microsoft product facts (per Microsoft Learn, checked September 2026 — verify before you design):

Generation 1

  • Connects to Azure via a Microsoft-managed ExpressRoute circuit; the private cloud is not inside your VNet.
  • Host SKUs: AV36, AV36P, AV48, AV52 — plus AV64 as an expansion cluster on an existing seed cluster.
  • vSAN OSA (Original Storage Architecture) on the base SKUs.
  • Azure-native controls like NSGs don't apply directly to the private cloud; security is NSX plus routed Azure services.

Generation 2

  • Deploys directly inside an Azure Virtual Network; VNet peering and Azure service connectivity work without extra networking.
  • Runs on the AV64 SKU only — minimum three AV64 hosts, deployable directly (no seed cluster).
  • vSAN ESA (Express Storage Architecture).
  • Supports NSGs against the private cloud, optional Private DNS resolution, and Availability Zone selection. Available in a growing list of regions, not all.

My architectural recommendation (not a Microsoft product statement): for a greenfield AVS evaluation, assess Gen2 first — subject to regional availability, workload requirements, feature compatibility and any architectural constraints. Choose Gen1 when an existing deployment, a required feature, or a region without Gen2 makes it necessary.

Either way, decide early. Microsoft's guidance is blunt: moving between generations requires workload migration. It is not a setting you toggle later — getting it wrong means migrating twice.

The Shared Responsibility Line — and Where It Cuts You

Microsoft operates and maintains the Azure infrastructure and the VMware platform: bare-metal hosts, and the deploy, patch and upgrade of ESXi, vCenter, vSAN and NSX. Microsoft also supports VMware HCX on AVS. That is real operational load lifted off your team.

What stays yours: applications, VMs, operating systems, data, NSX network and firewall configuration, identity and RBAC, security policy, compliance, backup and BCDR tooling (Microsoft's partner ecosystem here includes VMware Live Site Recovery, JetStream, Zerto and others) — and the one that surprises people — monitoring consumption and scaling host capacity to meet demand while balancing performance, resilience and cost. On Gen2, Azure-native controls such as NSGs against the private cloud also become yours to design.

In an owned datacenter, running out of headroom is a slow, visible problem: someone notices the cluster is full and starts a six-week procurement. In AVS, adding a host is an API call — which is exactly the risk. A poorly-governed AVS environment scales its own bill. The classic failure mode: a "temporary" capacity add to survive a quarter-end becomes permanent because nobody owns the decision to scale it back down.

Before you sign: walk the responsibility matrix

Confirm ownership for security, operations and compliance before adoption, line by line, against the published Microsoft-vs-customer responsibility matrix. "We assumed Microsoft had that" is not a finding you want in your first audit.

Taking the Decision to the Board

The board question isn't "did you migrate VMware." It's "what did this commit us to, and for how long."

A platform owner who has done the work walks in with three things: a fully-loaded comparison — the platform you run today versus AVS versus native, not a per-VM rate card; a one-line answer to "why not just refactor everything" that names the deadline and the workloads that can't move yet; and a dated, funded modernization backlog for whatever lands on AVS as a bridge.

Resist the urge to declare a single financial winner. Which option has the lowest three-year cost depends on migration complexity, VMware and VCF licensing, hardware-refresh timing, datacenter costs, application-remediation effort, dual-running periods, skills, security redesign, reserved capacity and Azure commitments — and those vary by estate. Azure VMware Solution is a strong answer to a specific question: we must leave this datacenter faster than we can safely re-architect, and enough of the estate has real VMware dependencies that a lift is the lower-risk path. Where that is the question, it is the right tool.

Where it isn't — where the estate is small, or modernization is already funded and scheduled — paying AVS host rates to keep running vSphere is the expensive version of not deciding.

Executive Conclusion

A VMware renewal is a platform-strategy trigger, not a licensing renewal to sign.

Azure VMware Solution earns its place when workload constraints, migration risk and business timing point to it — a hard datacenter-exit date, genuine VMware dependencies, high refactoring risk. It is one landing zone among several, not the automatic answer, and where it is used as a bridge it needs a funded exit.

Native Azure often fits better where modernization is funded, the workload has no real VMware dependency, or the five-year target state is VMware-free — accepting the higher upfront migration and change cost.

Neither vendor's platform is obsolete, and neither is automatically superior. The right answer depends on business constraints and workload characteristics, and it is rarely the same answer for the whole estate.

The goal is not to move VMware somewhere else. It is to make a deliberate platform decision, workload by workload, while balancing deadline, risk, economics and the long-term cloud architecture.
